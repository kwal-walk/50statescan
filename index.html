Background

What is Food Is Medicine?

The American Heart Association's Health Care × Food initiative defines Food Is Medicine (FIM) as "the provision of healthy food… to treat, manage or prevent diet-related health conditions in a way that is integrated with and paid for by the health care sector." Patients eligible for FIM services are referred to these services by their health care professional, health care organization, or health insurance plan.

FIM represents a meaningful shift in how the health care sector thinks about food — not just as a social determinant of health, but also as an active, reimbursable component of clinical care. Rather than relying solely on nutrition counseling or general healthy eating guidelines, FIM delivers specific food-based interventions that are prescribed for, tailored to, and integrated into a patient's broader care plan.

To date, FIM has demonstrated the potential to improve health outcomes, address health disparities, reduce food insecurity, and reduce health care utilization and costs.

Common FIM Interventions

FIM can take many forms. The most widely implemented include:

🍽️

Medically Tailored Meals (MTMs)

Prepared meals, often home-delivered, developed by Registered Dietitians to meet the specific clinical needs of individual patients

🥗

Medically Supportive Meals (MSMs)

Similar to MTMs, but typically designed for general health conditions rather than a specific individual's clinical needs

🛒

Medically Tailored Groceries (MTGs)

Unprepared healthy foods — often home-delivered or provided via voucher — tailored to a patient's specific dietary requirements

🛍️

Medically Supportive Groceries (MSGs)

Similar to MTGs, but designed for general conditions rather than individual-specific needs

🥕

Produce Prescriptions (PRx)

Vouchers or cards prescribed by health care providers that can be exchanged for fruits and vegetables

Other Approaches

Other approaches are less commonly thought of as FIM but may still be useful and may overlap with more traditional FIM approaches. Such approaches include:

💰

Incentive Programs

Programs that provide financial means of encouragement to purchase healthier foods, for example providing additional thirty cents in monetary food assistance for every dollar spent on produce

📉

Disincentive Programs

Programs that provide financial means of discouragement to purchase unhealthy foods, for example adding thirty cents to the cost of every dollar spent on unhealthy foods such as sugar-sweetened beverages

📚

Nutrition Education

General instruction provided by a health care professional, such as a RD, on how to manage nutrition-related problems

Not all forms of nutrition education are considered FIM; however, many FIM interventions incorporate some level of nutrition education to better serve participants

👩‍⚕️

Nutrition Counseling

Personalized and interactive guidance provided by a health care professional, such as a RD, on how to manage nutrition-related problems

Not all forms of nutrition counseling are considered FIM; however, FIM interventions may incorporate some level of nutrition counseling to better serve participants

Terminology

Food Is Medicine vs. Food As Medicine

These two terms are often used interchangeably, but they carry distinct meanings — and the difference matters for policy, research, and practice.

FIM — Food Is Medicine

A Health Care Sector Framework

  • More frequently used by health insurers, health systems, and funders in the health care space
  • Refers specifically to reimbursable, food-based interventions integrated into the health care system
  • Emphasizes clinical and financial structure: prescribed, delivered, and paid for as part of medical care
  • Includes programs such as medically tailored meals, produce prescriptions, and medically tailored groceries
FAM — Food As Medicine

A Broader Public Health Concept

  • More frequently used by nutrition professionals, public health organizations, and researchers
  • Refers to the broader concept of integrating nutrition into health care
  • Encompasses a wide range of approaches to using diet as a tool for health promotion and disease prevention
  • May include dietary guidance, culinary medicine, and general nutrition-focused health care

Both approaches have merit and play important roles in today's health care landscape. FIM is gaining traction — but significant gaps in federal funding and coverage remain

The Landscape

Who Is Driving FIM?

FIM interventions are funded and delivered by a diverse mix of federal programs, private payers, and health systems — though significant gaps remain, particularly at the federal level.

🍎

GusNIP (Federal)

The Gus Schumacher Nutrition Incentive Program funds incentive programs, produce prescriptions, and cooperative agreements at the federal level via USDA grants.

🚗

Meals on Wheels (Federal)

Meals on Wheels delivers 244 million meals to 2.6 million seniors annually. The meals are designed to be nutritious and age-appropriate, and the program also offers nutrition education and counseling.

🧓

Medicare — Limited Coverage

FIM is not currently covered by Medicare. Medicare Advantage plans may offer some grocery assistance, but these are generally not structured as true FIM interventions. Medicare does cover medical nutrition therapy for patients with diabetes and end-stage renal disease, but this typically consists of RD counseling alone.

🏥

Private Payers & Health Systems

The majority of FIM interventions have historically been funded by private payers, health insurance companies, and nonprofit or community-based organizations.

📈

Medicaid — A Growing Role

An increasing number of states are incorporating nutrition-related services into their Medicaid programs through §1115 demonstration waivers, §1915 waivers, and ILOS — the focus of this 50-state scan.

🔬

Research — Underfunded

FIM research is significantly underfunded at the NIH, creating a gap between early evidence and the robust evidence base needed to drive broader policy adoption.

The Policy Tools

§1115, §1915, and ILOS

Three distinct Medicaid authorities allow states to cover nutrition services beyond what traditional Medicaid state plans permit. Each has unique rules, time limits, and oversight requirements.

§1115 Waivers

Section 1115 Demonstration Waivers

Section 1115 of the Social Security Act allows the Secretary of Health and Human Services (HHS) to approve experimental, pilot, or demonstration projects aligned with Medicaid's objectives. States use §1115 waivers to experiment with state-specific policy approaches to improve Medicaid services. Demonstrations must be budget-neutral, consistent with federal policy, and continually evaluated for impact and effectiveness. If approved, they last five years and can be extended for an additional three to five years.

§1915 Waivers

Section 1915 Home & Community-Based Services (HCBS) Waivers

Section 1915 waivers allow states to offer HCBS that help keep eligible Medicaid beneficiaries in their homes or communities rather than institutional settings. The most common types for nutrition services are §1915(c) and §1915(i). If approved, these waivers last two, three, or five years and can be extended for an additional five years.

✅ 43 states have approved §1915 waivers that include nutrition-related services View §1915 Map →
ILOS

In Lieu of Services and Settings (ILOS)

ILOS allow Medicaid Managed Care Organizations (MCOs) to provide appropriate alternative benefits in place of traditional state Medicaid benefits. Unlike §1115 and §1915 waivers, which require federal approval, ILOS are approved at the state level. They must be cost-effective and medically appropriate. ILOS do not have strict time limits, making them a flexible and increasingly popular tool for states.
