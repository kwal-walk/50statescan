Economic Evidence for Food Is Medicine

A review of cost-effectiveness and return-on-investment studies  ·  Full literature review available below

Scope of This Review

Why Focus on Cost-Effectiveness?

Clinical evidence for FIM interventions is growing. But to drive Medicaid policy, states and payers need economic evidence — and that evidence is still limited.

"While the health benefits of Food Is Medicine programs are increasingly recognized, the American Heart Association has highlighted that formal economic analyses — including cost-effectiveness studies and return-on-investment analyses — remain a significant gap in the FIM evidence base, hindering broader policy adoption and payer uptake."
— Synthesis of AHA Health Care × Food initiative reports and FIM economic literature

Methodology

Two Research Approaches

The economic evidence base for FIM draws on two distinct methodological traditions — each with its own strengths and limitations.

Simulation / Modeling

What it is

Uses mathematical models to project costs and outcomes under hypothetical scenarios, often drawing on existing clinical trial data or population statistics.

Strengths

  • Can project long-term outcomes beyond typical study follow-up periods
  • Can model policy scenarios at the state or national level
  • Can produce cost-per-QALY (ICER) estimates useful for policy comparisons

Limitations

  • Results are only as valid as the underlying model assumptions
  • May not capture real-world implementation challenges
Observational

What it is

Analyzes real-world data from programs already in operation, comparing outcomes between participants and comparable non-participants.

Strengths

  • Reflects actual program implementation and real populations
  • Can capture outcomes across diverse settings and health conditions
  • Often uses large administrative datasets (claims, EMR)

Limitations

  • Cannot fully rule out selection bias or confounding
  • Rarely produce ICER estimates directly comparable across studies

Important Caveats

Limitations of the Evidence Base

The economic case for FIM is promising — but the evidence base has meaningful limitations that should temper overly strong conclusions.

  • Small and heterogeneous samples. Many observational studies rely on relatively small participant groups, limiting statistical power and generalizability.
  • Short follow-up periods. Most studies track costs and outcomes over 6–12 months. The long-term economic impact of FIM is largely unknown.
  • Limited randomized evidence. Very few randomized controlled trials with economic outcomes exist; most evidence is observational or modeled.
  • Model dependency. Simulation-based ICERs depend heavily on assumptions about adherence, efficacy, and program costs — small changes in assumptions can substantially alter results.
  • Publication bias. Positive findings are more likely to be published; the true average effect may be more modest than the literature suggests.
  • Implementation variation. Programs differ in design, eligibility criteria, food type, and delivery model — making cross-study comparisons difficult and limiting the generalizability of any single estimate.
